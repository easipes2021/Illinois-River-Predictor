How It Works

Understanding the physics of the Illinois River and the AI that predicts it.

1. What is a Watershed?

A watershed is an area of land that channels all rainfall and snowmelt to a single outflow point. The Illinois River begins south of Fayetteville, Arkansas, and flows north, then west into Oklahoma.

This means that every drop of rain that falls on Springdale, Rogers, Bentonville, and Siloam Springs eventually makes its way into the Illinois River. If a massive thunderstorm hits Rogers, the river at Watts Bridge (OK) will flood a day later, even if it hasn't rained a single drop in Oklahoma.

To accurately predict the river, we cannot just look at the river itself. We have to divide the entire Northwest Arkansas region into 26 distinct "HUC-12" sub-watersheds and measure the exact rainfall inside each one of them.

A stylized view of tributary networks funneling into a main river stem.

2. Hydrologic Routing & Time Lags

Water does not teleport. It takes time for water to flow over the ground, into a creek, down a tributary, and into the main river stem.

The "Time Lag" Matrix

  • Instant Impact (0 - 3 Hours) Rain falling directly on the main river valley causes an immediate spike in water levels.
  • Creek Routing (6 - 12 Hours) Rain falling in urban areas like Springdale takes hours to travel down Osage Creek before it violently crashes into the Illinois River.
  • Soil Saturation (Days to Weeks) If it rained heavily last week, the ground is completely saturated (acting like a wet sponge). New rain will instantly run off into the river. If it has been a dry summer, the ground will absorb the first 2 inches of rain like a dry sponge, and the river won't move at all.

3. The Machine Learning Predictor

The Illinois River Predictor doesn't use static physics equations. It uses XGBoost, an advanced machine learning algorithm, to constantly learn how the river behaves.

How the AI "Thinks"

Every hour, the system downloads exact radar rainfall estimates and current river heights. The Spatial Routing Engine groups the 26 tributary creeks into 4 distinct geographic zones.

We then create a "Time-Lag Matrix"—giving the AI thousands of columns of data containing what the rain was doing 3 hours ago, 6 hours ago, and 24 hours ago in each of those 4 zones.

The XGBoost AI analyzes years of this historical data to find the hidden mathematical patterns. It learns that "2 inches of rain in the Osage Zone, combined with high soil saturation, usually results in a 14-foot spike at the Highway 59 gauge exactly 12 hours later." It then outputs those predictions to the dashboard.
